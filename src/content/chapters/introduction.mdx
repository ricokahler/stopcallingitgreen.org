A solar panel in a field is a very boring object.

It doesn't roar. It doesn't smoke. It doesn't need a coal train, a tanker, a refinery, a pipeline, a drilling lease, or a Navy escort through somebody else's strait. Light lands on it, electrons move, and power comes out, for twenty-five or thirty years, mostly unattended.

Somehow this became the suspicious thing.

## How A Machine Got A Personality

We took one of the plainest energy machines people have ever built and gave it a political color. We called it "green energy," and that name has done more damage than most of its opponents ever managed.

"Green" makes a power plant sound like a moral position. It quietly swaps the question. Instead of "does this make cheap, reliable power?" the listener hears "am I the kind of person who likes green energy?" Roughly half the country reasonably answers that no, they are not that kind of person, thanks.

That isn't because half the country hates electricity. People want the lights on, a smaller bill, a warm house in January, and a country that doesn't get yanked around by a war in a shipping lane they had to look up. Cheap, domestic, fuel-free power is good at exactly those things. But once a machine gets a color, people stop looking at the machine.

The competition got better names. Oil became "energy." Gas became "natural." Coal became "baseload." Pipelines became "infrastructure." The fossil system got to dress up as the practical world itself, and solar got stuck in the costume.

## The Costume Doesn't Fit

Here's what makes the costume look silly: the places building the most of this stuff are not the places the costume suggests.

Texas generates more wind power than any other state and has become the country's solar and battery leader too. Iowa gets more than half its electricity from wind. Kansas, Oklahoma, and the Dakotas are not far behind. Nobody in those statehouses is taking orders from a climate nonprofit. They build it because it's cheap, it's fast to put up, and the landowners get a check.

So the thing being sold to half the country as a coastal lifestyle product is, on the ground, largely a Texas and Great Plains business. That gap between the label and the hardware is what this book is about.

## One Standard

This isn't a book asking you to become a green person. I don't care what you drive or whether you recycle, and some climate messaging makes me want to throw my phone too.

It's a book about one standard: if a new machine has to answer for its costs, the old one answers for its costs too.

If solar gets subsidies, let's count subsidies, including the oil and gas tax breaks old enough that nobody calls them subsidies anymore. If solar uses land, let's talk about land, including the tens of millions of acres of corn America grows to burn as fuel. If solar doesn't work at night, let's talk about night, and about the February week in Texas when gas plants froze. If clean energy is a money grab, let's follow the money. All of it.

I'm not asking you to be less skeptical. I'm asking you to be skeptical in both directions. The half-skepticism on offer right now notices a solar tax credit and says "aha." Then it looks at a century-old fuel industry with permanent tax breaks, a lobbying budget north of a hundred million dollars a year, its own classroom materials, and a hit TV show, and says, "Well, that's just energy."

That isn't skepticism. It's brand loyalty with a podcast voice.

## What This Book Is And Isn't

Solar isn't perfect. Neither is wind, or batteries, or the grid. Every energy system has tradeoffs, because energy is the part of civilization where physics walks into politics and asks for a land-use permit. I'll try to be straight about the weak spots: China's grip on solar manufacturing, the real cost of keeping lights on after sunset, rooftop-solar scams, the towns that don't want a project next door. An argument that hides its weak spots isn't worth passing along.

What I'm going after is narrower. It's the story that made fossil fuels feel like reality and everything else feel like ideology. That story was built, some of it on purpose and with a budget, and anything built can be inspected.

The first chapters look at the machine and the money. The middle ones look at how fossil fuels came to feel like common sense: classroom handouts, ad campaigns, a prestige TV drama, and one very effective adjective. Then come the serious objections, land and night, followed by what's actually at stake: a strait half a world away that just showed Americans exactly how exposed they are, and a rival that treats power lines as national strategy.

Start with the electron. It's the only participant in this argument with no opinion.
