Imagine energy politics getting boring.

Not boring because nothing matters. Boring the way water pressure is boring. The lights work, the bill makes sense, the grid gets upgraded before it fails instead of after, the cheapest useful power gets built, and nobody has to pretend a fuel lobby is a freedom movement.

We're a long way from that. Somewhere along the line, Americans let electricity become a team jersey. We let "energy independence" mean drilling harder inside a market priced by strangers, and we let our skepticism get lazy in exactly one direction. None of that happened by accident. A lot of it was paid for.

## Keep The Skepticism

The instinct to ask who's getting paid is a good one. A free country needs people who check who gets the subsidy, who wrote the rule, and who eats the hidden cost. Nothing in this book asks you to give that up.

It asks you to aim it evenly. If a tax credit for a battery makes your neck hot but a tax break written in 1926 feels like the natural order, ask why. If a solar farm looks political and a refinery looks like scenery, ask why. If a TV oilman sounds like the only adult in the room, ask who benefits from you feeling that way.

You don't have to become a "green person." You don't have to dislike oil workers, lecture farmers, or put a slogan in your bio. You just have to get harder to fool. In practice that means a few follow-up questions:

- When someone says solar wastes land: compared with what? The 30-odd million acres of corn we grow to burn?
- When someone says clean energy is a money grab: who pays for the oil industry's lobbyists, ad campaigns, and classroom materials?
- When someone says renewables can't keep the lights on: which plants actually failed the last time the lights went out?
- When someone says energy independence: independent from what, exactly? Because the 2026 oil shock found Americans at the gas pump whether the oil came from Texas or not.

## Vote For The Wire

Politically, the short version is: vote for the wire, not the lobbyist.

Look for candidates, of either party, who talk about energy as a machine instead of a costume. People who want to build transmission and fix the interconnection backlog. People who'd let a farmer lease land to a solar developer without a county commission deciding it offends the view. People who want factories for panels, batteries, and transformers here instead of in China. People who are fine with nuclear and geothermal where they make sense, and who treat gas as a tool rather than a religion.

Be wary of anyone whose energy plan is mostly an enemy.

Oil and gas workers deserve respect. The lobby doesn't deserve obedience. Skeptical Americans deserve respect too, and the people making money off their suspicion don't deserve a blank check.

The United States has the land, the sun, the wind, the engineers, the capital, and the restless practical streak to build a far better energy system than the one it has. It won't be perfect. Infrastructure never is. But it can be cheaper, sturdier, more of it can be built at home, and less of it can be held hostage by a strait, a cartel, or an industry that taught us to confuse its profits with our freedom.

The sun isn't a Democrat. A transmission line isn't a sermon. A panel in a field isn't a culture war.

It's just power. We can afford to be adults about it.

## The Shoulders Under This

This book is a synthesis of people who've done the work longer and better than I have. The main trails run through Climate Town, Hank Green, Technology Connections, Undecided with Matt Ferrell, and JerryRigEverything, and through the people who publish the numbers: EIA, NREL, DOE, USGS, USDA, FERC and NERC, Berkeley Lab, Grid Strategies, the IEA, Lazard, OpenSecrets, Global Energy Monitor, the Yale Program on Climate Change Communication, and the reporters and researchers linked throughout. ## A Note On How This Was Made

This book was made with heavy AI assistance and human responsibility. The first version came together fast. This edition was substantially rewritten: the structure was rebuilt, the claims were re-checked against primary sources where they exist, numbers were corrected or updated through 2026, and places where the first draft overreached were pulled back. Language models helped draft, organize, and fact-check. They don't make anything true, and neither does a confident tone. If you find an error, the whole thing is [open source on GitHub](https://github.com/ricokahler/stopcallingitgreen.org), and corrections are welcome.

The project is self-funded and free, and there's nothing for sale at the end of it.
