Imagine energy politics getting boring.

Not boring because nothing matters; boring the way water pressure is. The lights work, the bill makes sense, the grid gets upgraded before it fails instead of after, the cheapest useful power gets built, and nobody has to pretend a fuel lobby is a freedom movement.

We're a long way from that. Somewhere along the line, we let electricity become a team jersey. We let "energy independence" mean drilling harder inside a market priced by strangers, and we let our skepticism run in only one direction. Some of that was habit. A lot of it was paid for.

## Keep The Skepticism

The instinct to ask who's getting paid is a good one, and nothing in this book asks you to give it up. A free country needs people who check who gets the subsidy, who wrote the rule, and who eats the hidden cost. If a tax credit for a battery makes your neck hot, fine. Just ask why a tax break written in 1926 doesn't.

You don't have to become a "green person," dislike oil workers, lecture farmers, or put a slogan in your bio. In practice, being hard to fool mostly means keeping a few follow-up questions in your back pocket:

- When someone says solar wastes land: compared with what? The 30-odd million acres of corn we grow to burn?
- When someone says clean energy is a money grab: who pays for the oil industry's lobbyists, ad campaigns, and classroom materials?
- When someone says renewables can't keep the lights on: which plants actually failed the last time the lights went out?
- When someone says energy independence: independent from what, exactly? Because the 2026 oil shock found all of us at the gas pump whether the oil came from Texas or not.

## Vote For The Wire

Politically, the short version is: vote for the wire, not the lobbyist.

Look for candidates, of either party, who talk about energy as a machine instead of a costume. People who want to build transmission (the big lines that move power between regions) and clear the interconnection backlog (the line of new projects waiting for permission to plug into the grid). People who'd protect a farmer's right to lease land for solar and give the neighbors a fair hearing, not a blanket veto. People who want factories for panels, batteries, and transformers here instead of in China. People who are fine with nuclear and geothermal where they make sense, and who treat gas as a tool rather than a religion.

Be wary of anyone whose energy plan is mostly an enemy.

Oil and gas workers deserve respect. The lobby doesn't deserve obedience. Skeptical Americans deserve respect too, and the industries that profit from aiming that suspicion in one direction don't deserve a blank check.

The United States has the land, the sun, the wind, the engineers, the capital, and the restless practical streak to build a far better energy system than the one we've got. It won't be perfect. Infrastructure never is. But it can be cheaper and sturdier, more of it can be built at home, and less of it can be held hostage by a strait, a cartel, or an industry that taught us to confuse its profits with our freedom.

The sun isn't a Democrat. A transmission line isn't a sermon. It's just power, and we're allowed to treat it that way.

## The Shoulders Under This

This book is built on the work of people who've been doing it longer and better than I have. Most of the numbers come from the people whose job is to publish them: the Energy Information Administration, the Department of Energy and its national labs, FERC and NERC, USDA, USGS, BLS, Berkeley Lab, the IEA, Lazard, OpenSecrets, Global Energy Monitor, and Grid Strategies. Many of the trails started with creators who explain this stuff well, especially Technology Connections, Hank Green, Undecided with Matt Ferrell, JerryRigEverything, and Climate Town, and with the reporters and researchers linked throughout. Every chapter has its sources at the bottom. Check them.

## A Note On How This Was Made

This book was made with heavy AI assistance and human responsibility. The first version came together fast. This edition was substantially rewritten: the structure was rebuilt, the claims were re-checked against primary sources where they exist, numbers were corrected or updated through 2026, and places where the first draft overreached were pulled back. Language models helped draft, organize, and fact-check. They don't make anything true, and neither does a confident tone. If you find an error, the whole thing is [open source on GitHub](https://github.com/ricokahler/stopcallingitgreen.org), and corrections are welcome.

The project is self-funded and free, and there's nothing for sale at the end of it.
